Investigation 01 · Policy file

Banksia North Growth Corridor

Fictional policy scenario

The proposal would permit approximately 4,000 new homes, create a bus interchange, develop a commercial and light-industrial precinct, construct flood-mitigation infrastructure, remove part of an existing wetland buffer, and require substantial public and private expenditure.

It may improve housing supply, transport and employment. It may also create environmental, financial, infrastructure and flood-risk consequences. A policy can benefit one group while transferring costs or risks to another; public interest cannot be established by merely counting supporters and opponents.

Decision test

Sound public decision-making examines evidence, affected interests, legal authority, feasibility, cost, rights, long-term consequences and alternative options.

Proposed benefits

  • Increased housing supply
  • New employment
  • Improved transport connections
  • Additional commercial activity
  • Flood-mitigation works
  • Expanded local government revenue base

Possible risks

  • Loss of wetland habitat
  • Increased construction traffic
  • Pressure on schools and health services
  • Public infrastructure costs
  • Development in flood-exposed areas
  • Private financial gain influencing debate
  • Unequal impacts across communities

Unresolved questions

  • How reliable are housing-demand forecasts?
  • Who will pay for major infrastructure?
  • Will housing be affordable?
  • How will flood risk be assessed?
  • Can wetland damage be avoided or reduced?
  • How many permanent jobs will be created?
  • Which communities face greatest disruption?
  • What monitoring and accountability will apply?

Investigation 02 · Pathways briefing

How Does Influence Reach Government?

Civic participation

Civic participation means actions through which people contribute to public life and try to shape collective decisions. It includes voting, contacting representatives, joining organisations, attending consultations, writing submissions, organising petitions, peaceful protest, public education, media commentary, volunteering and standing for election.

Participation creates an opportunity for political influence; it does not create an automatic right to receive a preferred outcome. Representative responsiveness requires decision-makers to listen and give reasons, but representatives must also weigh competing evidence, rights and the wider public interest.

Interest and pressure groups

An interest group is an organised body advancing shared concerns or objectives. A pressure group emphasises attempts to change public policy through advocacy rather than seeking to form government itself. Groups may represent industries, professions, workers, environmental causes, consumers, faith communities, residents, people affected by policy or broad public-interest causes.

Self-interest is not automatically illegitimate. A business may offer valuable implementation expertise while possessing a vested interest in financial gain. A community organisation may describe genuine harm while still presenting one perspective. Pluralism expects multiple organised voices to compete and contribute, but unequal resources can produce unequal access.

Lobbying

Lobbying is organised communication intended to influence a public decision. A lobbyist might arrange meetings with ministers, advisers, MPs or senators; prepare policy briefs and research reports; propose amendments; respond to consultation; or coordinate a public campaign. Lobbying is not automatically corruption.

Legitimate lobbying can provide technical knowledge, lived experience, implementation information and warnings about unintended consequences. Concern arises when influence relies on secrecy, misleading evidence, undisclosed funding, improper benefits, privileged access, a conflict of interest, or pressure that bypasses accountable processes. Transparency makes influence visible; disclosure identifies relevant funding, relationships and interests.

Public consultation and submissions

Public consultation invites affected people and organisations to provide information before a decision. It improves legitimacy only when participation can realistically affect consideration, rather than being used to decorate a predetermined result. A strong submission identifies the issue, states a position, explains reasons, supplies evidence, acknowledges limitations, identifies consequences and proposes practical recommendations.

Decision-makers should seek corroboration: independent support for a claim from another credible source or method. Repetition by connected campaigners is not the same as corroboration.

Petitions and peaceful protest

A petition can demonstrate public concern and place an issue before decision-makers. Yet signatures do not prove that a policy will work; wording may simplify complexity; duplicate or poorly verified support reduces reliability; and minority rights cannot always be settled by majority preference.

Peaceful protest can draw attention, demonstrate commitment, amplify excluded voices and create political pressure. Disruption may affect public support, slogans may oversimplify, and crowd size does not establish a factual claim. Participation should remain lawful and non-violent; this investigation does not provide jurisdiction-specific legal advice.

Media, framing and amplification

Media can investigate, explain, expose misconduct, amplify voices and perform agenda-setting by influencing which issues receive sustained attention. Media framing is the selection and emphasis that shapes how audiences interpret an issue—for example, presenting the corridor mainly as a housing solution or mainly as an environmental risk.

Social media can reduce participation barriers but can also accelerate misinformation (false information shared without necessarily intending harm), disinformation (false information deliberately created or spread to deceive), selective evidence, outrage, harassment, anonymous influence and manipulated popularity. Attention is evidence of reach, not necessarily truth.

Grassroots action and astroturfing

A grassroots campaign is genuinely organised from within a community. Astroturfing describes a campaign designed to appear community-led while being substantially organised or financed by a concealed powerful interest. The public appearance may be similar, so funding disclosure, source identification and accountability matter.

A transparent campaign can still advance self-interest, and a volunteer campaign can still circulate weak claims. Analysts must judge evidence and conduct rather than awarding automatic credibility according to group type.

Investigation 05 · Strategy laboratory

Strategy Lab — Choosing How to Participate

Influence methods serve different purposes. A responsible strategy connects an audience, evidence and safeguard to the task rather than assuming that the most visible or expensive method is always best.

Contacting a representative

Strengths: direct communication, explanation of local consequences and opportunities for questions.

Limitations: one message may receive limited attention, access varies and unsupported assertions may be unpersuasive.

Public submission

Strengths: creates a formal record, permits detailed evidence and exposes recommendations to scrutiny.

Limitations: demands time and literacy, may attract little public attention, and quality matters more than length.

Petition

Strengths: demonstrates visible support, assists agenda-setting and attracts attention.

Limitations: may simplify complexity, numbers do not prove effectiveness and signatories may have different reasons.

Media campaign

Strengths: reaches broad audiences, amplifies neglected evidence and can increase accountability.

Limitations: framing may distort, emotion may displace evidence and short news cycles reduce depth.

Peaceful protest

Strengths: visible participation, amplification of excluded voices and urgency in agenda-setting.

Limitations: disruption may reduce support, slogans may replace analysis and visibility does not prove accuracy.

Private lobbying meeting

Strengths: detailed discussion, technical questioning and rapid communication.

Limitations: unequal access, reduced transparency and risks of undisclosed commitments or conflicts.

Grassroots organising

Strengths: builds local knowledge, sustains participation and distributes leadership.

Limitations: requires time, risks volunteer fatigue and may struggle against heavily funded campaigns.

Three-action influence plan

Choose three distinct methods in sequence. Every action needs a purpose, audience, evidence, limitation and transparency safeguard.

Investigation 06 · Media framing desk

Media Desk — Same Policy, Different Story

Media framing is the selection and emphasis of information that shapes how an audience interprets an issue. Frames emerge through headline wording, image choice, source selection, included or omitted statistics, sequence, emotional examples, labels and assumptions about causes or consequences.

Framing does not automatically make a report false. Accurate facts can still construct a partial perspective. An analyst therefore asks both “Is this claim supported?” and “What understanding does this selection encourage?”

Report A

4,000 Homes Blocked by Environmental Campaigners

Focuses on housing need and delay, mentions the wetland briefly, and quotes a developer and housing advocate.

Report B

Wetlands Sacrificed for Private Profit

Focuses on environmental loss and developer profit, without explaining affordable-housing conditions or flood works.

Report C

Growth Corridor Promises Jobs—But How Many Will Last?

Distinguishes construction employment from permanent positions and requests supporting data.

Report D

Banksia North Weighs Housing, Flood Risk and Infrastructure Cost

Presents competing evidence and identifies uncertainty without pretending every claim has equal evidentiary strength.

Investigation 07 · Verification file

Verification File — Viral Does Not Mean Verified

Misinformation is false or misleading information shared without established intent to deceive. Disinformation is false or misleading information deliberately produced or distributed to deceive. Analysts must not infer deliberate intent merely because a claim is inaccurate; the supplied evidence must establish deception.

Post 1
“Independent flood experts proved the project is completely safe.”

File note: The Institute said further modelling was required.

Post 2
“Developers will make millions, so every claim they make must be false.”

File note: Financial interest requires scrutiny but does not automatically invalidate evidence.

Post 3
“8,000 residents signed the petition. That proves the wetland must be protected.”

File note: The petition demonstrates concern but cannot alone prove the policy conclusion.

Post 4
“Construction will create 2,000 jobs.”

File note: Duration, method and whether jobs are new, full-time or local are unspecified.

Post 5
“This community campaign is completely grassroots.”

Additional file: Most advertising was secretly financed by an organisation connected to a commercial competitor—possible astroturfing and an undisclosed interest.

Investigation 08 · Transparency and power audit

Power Audit — Whose Influence Can Be Seen?

Transparency does not require every political conversation to occur publicly. Decision-makers need space to receive confidential information, test proposals, negotiate and protect legitimate privacy.

Secrecy undermines accountability when the public cannot identify who sought influence, whose interests were represented, what evidence was supplied, whether financial interests or commitments existed, or whether access was distributed fairly.

Conflict of interest

A conflict exists when a personal, financial or organisational interest may improperly influence—or reasonably appear to influence—a public responsibility. It does not automatically prove misconduct. It should be identified, disclosed, managed and considered when evidence is evaluated.

Record 1

A public submission contains data, funding disclosure and methodology.

Record 2

A private technical meeting appears in a public ministerial diary with participants and topic identified.

Record 3

An expensive advertising campaign is funded through an organisation refusing to identify major donors.

Record 4

A community petition has clear wording and verified local signatories but no policy analysis.

Record 5

A Northline-funded research paper discloses its funding and publishes methods for scrutiny.

Record 6

A public official accepts a personal benefit from a company seeking approval.

Six-record integrity audit

Deliberation Board — What Should Happen Next?

Do not begin with a simple approve-or-reject vote. Organise the strongest evidence, uncertainty, missing voices, conflicts, corrections and safeguards first. Deliberation tests whether a conclusion can survive comparison rather than merely reflecting which claim was heard most often.

Optional ethical reflection

Faith & Citizenship — Speaking with Courage and Responsibility

The Book of Esther includes an account of Esther using her unusual access to political authority to advocate for people facing grave danger. Her influence depended on access, courage, preparation, timing, truthful explanation, willingness to accept personal risk and responsibility towards others.

This account does not make Esther a modern lobbyist, provide a modern lobbying code or prove private access is always fair. Faith does not guarantee political success, no Growth Corridor position is presented as uniquely Christian, and private advocacy is not automatically improper. As ethical reflection rather than Australian law, the account prompts questions about using influence for service, responsibility when others lack access, courage in raising neglected harm, preparation, transparency and truthfulness.

Optional advanced analysis

Parliament House Prawn Challenge

“Suppose every citizen has the same legal right to contact government, but one organisation can hire researchers, purchase advertising and meet decision-makers repeatedly while another relies on volunteers. Is the process equal?”

Distinguish formal equality, substantive equality, expertise, financial resources, freedom of political communication, transparency, access, practical barriers, possible safeguards and the risk of government deciding whose voice deserves to be heard.
