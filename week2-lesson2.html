📖 Investigation File — Two Houses, One System

Section A — What is Parliament?

Parliament is the institution that debates public issues, examines government action and considers proposed laws. Constitutionally, the Australian Parliament has three parts: the King, represented federally by the Governor-General; the House of Representatives; and the Senate.

The Governor-General’s parliamentary role includes giving Royal Assent to Bills passed by both Houses. The detailed stages for passing a Bill belong to the next lesson. For now, remember that Parliament is not simply another word for government, and Cabinet is part of executive government—not a third parliamentary chamber. Courts interpret and apply law, but they are not part of Parliament.

Section B — Parliament and government

Parliament includes government, opposition, minor-party and independent parliamentarians. Government is normally formed by the party or coalition that commands the confidence of the House of Representatives. It develops policy and administers the country through ministers and federal departments.

Parliament debates, examines and authorises proposed laws. Opposition members question government decisions and present alternatives. The crossbench consists of minor-party and independent parliamentarians who sit apart from government and opposition benches. They may support, oppose or seek changes to proposals. Disagreement is a normal feature of representative democracy, not proof that the system has failed.

Section C — The House of Representatives

Australia is divided into geographic electorates. Voters in each electorate elect one member to the House. Electorates aim to contain broadly similar numbers of voters, although boundaries change as populations move. Members represent local communities while also making national decisions.

The party or coalition with the confidence of the House normally forms government, and the Prime Minister is normally a House member. Many government Bills begin there, and certain taxation and spending proposals must originate there. “Lower House” is a traditional label; it does not mean that the House is less important.

Section D — The Senate

Each state has equal representation in the Senate. The territories also elect senators, but have fewer senators than the states. A senator represents an entire state or territory rather than one local electorate. The Senate reviews proposed laws and government activity, so it is often called the “House of review”.

The Senate may agree, disagree, request changes or refer an issue for further investigation. Its committees can examine evidence and hear from affected groups. Governments do not always control a majority of Senate votes, so they may need support from opposition or crossbench senators. “Upper House” does not mean that the Senate is simply more powerful.

Section E — Why two types of representation?

The two Houses combine different democratic ideas. House representation is based broadly on population, while Senate representation gives each state equal numbers of state senators. Imagine one highly populated state and one less populated state: the first has more House electorates, but both have equal numbers of state senators.

Australians therefore receive local, population-based representation and state-based representation. Equal state representation protects smaller states, but students may reasonably debate whether it gives individual voters equal influence. Australia’s bicameral—two-chamber—design is a constitutional compromise between these principles.

Section F — Government, opposition and crossbench

Government proposes policies, introduces many Bills, administers federal departments and answers questions in Parliament. Opposition questions government decisions, proposes alternatives and tries to demonstrate that it could govern more effectively.

Crossbench parliamentarians may support, oppose or negotiate changes to proposals. They can be influential when neither major grouping has enough votes alone. Whatever their position, all elected members and senators have representative duties. A senator may raise local concerns, and a House member also considers state and national interests; representation overlaps even though each chamber has a distinct foundation.

Section G — Scrutiny and accountability

Scrutiny means careful examination. Parliament scrutinises through debate, questions to ministers, committee inquiries, examination of spending, proposed amendments, public reporting, and criticism by opposition and crossbench members. Accountability means requiring decision-makers to explain and defend their actions.

Scrutiny is not automatic obstruction. It can identify errors, hidden costs or unintended consequences and improve a proposal. Both Houses debate national issues, examine Bills and hold government to account. A second examination is valuable when it is careful and evidence-based, rather than delay for its own sake.

Section H — Why the Houses may disagree

The Houses may reach different conclusions because they represent people differently, their party composition may differ, senators may focus on state or territory effects, committees may uncover new evidence, or parliamentarians may disagree about cost, fairness and effectiveness.

Disagreement can delay a decision, but it can also prevent rushed law-making. Both Houses participate in ordinary federal law-making; neither is simply the boss. Their disagreement creates a need for explanation, negotiation and sometimes revision.
