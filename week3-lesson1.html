📖 Investigation File — The Australian Court Hierarchy

Section A — Why have a hierarchy?

A traffic offence does not need the same process as murder, a complex civil dispute or a constitutional challenge. Hierarchy supports specialisation, efficient allocation, appeal pathways, consistent interpretation and manageable workloads. “Higher” does not mean morally better; it usually means broader or more senior jurisdiction.

Section B — Jurisdiction

Jurisdiction is legal authority to hear and decide particular matters. It may depend on offence type and seriousness, civil complexity, location, state or federal law, and whether the matter is a trial or appeal. A court cannot hear any dispute it chooses. Australia has overlapping state, territory and federal systems—not one perfectly straight ladder.

Section C — Magistrates Courts

The Magistrates Court is the lowest general state level and deals with most court matters, including many summary offences and lower-value civil disputes. A magistrate, not a judge, usually presides without a jury. Serious indictable charges may have early procedural hearings here before trial in a higher court; this does not mean every case begins here.

Section D — District or County Courts

This intermediate level exists in some jurisdictions. Queensland calls it the District Court; Victoria uses County Court. It hears many serious criminal trials, larger civil disputes and some lower-court appeals. A judge presides, and some criminal trials use juries. Names and structures vary across Australia.

Section E — Supreme Courts

Each state and territory has a Supreme Court: its highest court. It hears the most serious state criminal matters, including murder, and major or complex civil matters. It also has an appellate function, often through a Court of Appeal. It is not Australia’s final appeal court; some decisions may reach the High Court with special leave.

Section F — The High Court

The High Court is Australia’s highest court. It interprets the Constitution and hears selected appeals involving significant legal questions. Appeals generally require special leave, so it hears relatively few cases and not every appeal. It does not normally serve as the first trial court for ordinary crimes, however dramatic they are.

Section G — Federal courts

Federal courts are not a rung inserted between state Supreme Courts and the High Court. The Federal Court hears particular federal matters such as Commonwealth administrative decisions, corporations, competition, bankruptcy, industrial matters and some migration judicial review. The Federal Circuit and Family Court deals with family law, divorce, parenting, and some migration and general federal matters. Detailed exceptions are beyond this Year 9 model.

Section H — Specialist courts and tribunals

Children’s Courts deal with children’s matters; Coroners Courts investigate certain deaths; Queensland also has a Land Court. Administrative tribunals often resolve disputes less formally. A tribunal is not necessarily a court even though it determines disputes. Specialist institutions develop expertise in particular subject areas.

Section I — Trials and appeals

A trial hears the original matter, evidence and legal arguments. An appeal asks a higher court to review an alleged legal, procedural or factual error. Appeal rights vary and permission may be required. An appeal is not automatically a complete new trial and is not available merely because someone dislikes losing. An appellate court may confirm, change or overturn a decision, or order another hearing.

Section J — Precedent

Precedent means relevant legal principles from higher-court decisions guide or bind lower courts within the hierarchy. This supports consistency, predictability and equality before law. Courts must decide whether an earlier case is sufficiently similar, and not every sentence spoken by a judge becomes binding law. Detailed precedent rules are simplified here.

Section K — Access and fairness

Court structure balances local access, expertise, cost, efficiency, review of error and consistency. Lower courts make everyday justice more accessible while appeals can correct serious error. Yet proceedings can still be costly, slow and difficult to understand—a problem explored later in Week 3.
