🐝 Mission 10 — Two Files, One Collision

Morgan allegedly drives dangerously and collides with another vehicle. Passenger Riley suffers a broken arm, misses work and has medical expenses.

File A: the state alleges a dangerous-driving offence. File B: Riley seeks compensation for injury and financial loss. A clerk has combined them.

📖 Investigation File — Different Pathways

Section A — Two purposes

Criminal justice asks whether the accused committed an offence prohibited by law. It responds to conduct treated by the state as an offence against the community, such as assault, stealing, dangerous driving, fraud, property damage or murder. Civil justice asks whether a party breached a duty or caused legally recognised loss requiring a remedy. It usually resolves disputes and repairs harm. Both concern fairness and responsibility, but pursue different outcomes.

Section B — Who brings the case?

Police investigate suspected offences, but not every investigation produces a charge. If legally appropriate, prosecution is conducted for the state or Crown; the charged person is the accused or defendant and is presumed innocent. A victim may be an important witness but is not usually the prosecuting party.

In civil proceedings, the plaintiff or applicant begins the claim and the defendant or respondent answers. Terms vary by court and proceeding. Both sides can present evidence and legal arguments.

Section C — Simplified criminal pathway
  1. Alleged offence.
  2. Police investigation.
  3. A charge may be laid.
  4. Accused appears in court.
  5. Prosecution presents its case.
  6. Accused may challenge it and present a defence.
  7. Court decides whether guilt is proven.
  8. If guilty, sentencing follows.

Procedure varies with offence and court. Conviction does not automatically mean imprisonment.

Section D — Simplified civil pathway
  1. Loss or infringement is alleged.
  2. Parties may communicate or negotiate.
  3. A claim may be filed.
  4. Defendant responds.
  5. Evidence and arguments are exchanged.
  6. Matter may settle or proceed to hearing.
  7. Court decides liability.
  8. If liable, a remedy may be ordered.

Many civil matters settle without a final hearing. Here “civil law” means disputes, not the different international tradition called a civil-law legal system.

Section E — Burden of proof

The burden of proof identifies who must prove the case. The prosecution generally bears it in a criminal matter, consistent with the presumption of innocence. The plaintiff generally bears it in a civil matter. An allegation alone does not usually make the responding party prove the entire opposite case. Legal exceptions exist beyond this Year 9 model.

Section F — Standard of proof

The standard of proof describes how convincing evidence must be. Criminal guilt must be proved beyond reasonable doubt. This higher standard reflects serious consequences including punishment, restricted liberty, reputation damage and long-term effects.

Civil liability is determined on the balance of probabilities: whether the claim is more likely than not after careful assessment, including the allegation’s seriousness. Neither standard is a fixed mathematical percentage.

Section G — Use the right language
CriminalCivil
Prosecution; accused or defendant; offence; guilty/not guilty; conviction; sentencePlaintiff/applicant; defendant/respondent; claim; liable/not liable; remedy; damages/compensation

“Not guilty” means guilt was not proved beyond reasonable doubt; it does not necessarily prove the alleged event never happened. Ordinary civil cases do not formally decide guilt or innocence.

Section H — Outcomes

Criminal outcomes include guilty or not guilty findings, acquittal, dismissal and—after conviction—lawful sentencing orders such as fines, community-based orders, licence disqualification or imprisonment. Sentence depends on legislation, the offence and offender.

Civil outcomes include liable, not liable, settlement, compensation, a declaration or an injunction requiring someone to do or stop doing something.

Section I — One event, two cases

The criminal issue is whether Morgan committed dangerous driving; the civil issue is whether Morgan is legally responsible for Riley’s losses. Parties, purposes, standards and outcomes differ, though evidence may overlap. Acquittal does not automatically prevent a civil claim, and civil liability does not create a criminal conviction.

Section J — Judges, magistrates and juries

Many criminal cases are decided by magistrates without juries; some serious trials use judge and jury. Most civil cases are decided by judges or magistrates. Civil juries are uncommon in many Australian jurisdictions but may exist in limited circumstances. Procedure depends on jurisdiction and dispute type.

Section K — Fairness and access

Both pathways seek fair procedure, but cost, delay, complexity, evidence difficulties, stress and unequal legal representation can obstruct justice. These challenges connect directly to the next lesson on access to justice.
