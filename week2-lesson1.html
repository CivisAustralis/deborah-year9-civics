🐝 Mission 5 — The Prime Minister Needs Advice

Parliament House has received many suggestions from Australians: stronger responses to dangerous driving, improved flood protection, more public transport and even free ice cream every Friday.

The Prime Minister has asked you to work as a junior policy adviser. Your mission is to decide which ideas concern the public interest—the wellbeing of the community—and investigate how a serious proposal may reach Cabinet.

Governments receive more ideas than they can act upon. Good advisers consider evidence, fairness, cost, responsibility and likely consequences. Let’s get busy as a bee and examine the reasons behind each choice.

🎯 Learning Intention

To understand how an identified public problem can develop into government policy before a proposed law is introduced into Parliament.

✅ Success Criteria

By the end, you should be able to:

  • identify several sources of policy and law-reform ideas;
  • explain what government departments and ministers do;
  • describe Cabinet’s role;
  • arrange a simplified policy-development pathway in order;
  • justify why some proposals should receive government attention; and
  • distinguish Cabinet approval from parliamentary law-making.

🗳️ Initial Decision — What Should Government Investigate?

Before reading the investigation file, select the two proposals you believe government should investigate first. A sound policy decision needs reasons, not merely personal preference.

📖 Investigation File — From an Idea to Cabinet

Important: This lesson presents a common, simplified pathway suitable for Year 9. Real policy development is not always identical or perfectly linear.

Section A — Where ideas come from

A policy is a plan for government action. Policy and law-reform ideas can begin with citizens, petitions and community campaigns, Members of Parliament, ministers, government departments, expert reports, parliamentary committees and advocacy organisations. Court decisions may expose problems in an existing law. Election commitments can promise a course of action, while emergencies such as floods, bushfires or public-health crises can reveal an urgent need.

Receiving an idea does not guarantee government action. Decision-makers must ask whether the issue is supported by evidence, falls within that level of government’s responsibility and deserves priority among many competing needs.

Section B — Identifying the problem

Before choosing a solution, policymakers must define the actual problem. With dangerous driving, the problem is not simply “cars are bad”. It may be a pattern of serious injuries linked to particular high-risk driving behaviour. A clear problem makes it possible to compare focused responses rather than adopting a broad rule that misses the cause.

Evidence matters because an appealing idea may not work as expected. Evidence could include crash data, hospital data, police reports, community experiences, expert advice and financial estimates. Policymakers should ask how reliable and representative the evidence is, who is affected and whether the proposed response is likely to reduce the harm.

Section C — Government departments

Government departments support the work of government in areas such as health, education, transport, defence and the environment. A portfolio is an area of responsibility managed by a minister. The department connected to that portfolio supplies specialist knowledge and ongoing administrative experience.

Departmental officials may research a problem, consult affected groups, examine legal options, estimate costs, identify likely consequences and prepare advice. Their role is to provide careful, impartial advice and help carry out lawful government decisions. Officials do not make the elected government’s political and policy choices; ministers and the government remain accountable for those decisions.

Section D — Ministers

Ministers are members of the government responsible for particular portfolios. A minister may examine departmental advice, request more work, consult colleagues and decide whether to propose that Cabinet consider an issue.

A responsible minister weighs evidence, cost, fairness, effects on communities, government priorities, legal and constitutional limits, and possible unintended consequences. For example, a safety measure may reduce one risk but impose an unfair cost elsewhere. The minister must explain both the expected benefit and the trade-offs, rather than presenting only the strongest argument in favour.

Section E — Cabinet

Cabinet is the central decision-making group of senior government ministers. It discusses major policy proposals, compares competing priorities, considers cost and public effect, decides whether the government will support a proposal, and may authorise further policy or legislative development. Cabinet may approve, reject or request changes and more evidence.

Cabinet does not pass an Act of Parliament. If legislation is required, the proposal must be developed and legally drafted as a Bill, then introduced into Parliament. Parliament considers, debates and votes on Bills. A Bill becomes an Act only after it passes the required parliamentary stages and receives assent.

Cabinet discussions are generally confidential so ministers can debate proposals before the government announces a collective decision. Ministers are then expected to support that decision publicly.

Section F — Not every problem needs a new law

Government may respond through education, funding, services, infrastructure, regulation, administrative change, better enforcement of existing law, or a new or amended law. Flood safety, for example, might be improved through warning infrastructure and public education without creating a new offence.

A proposed new law may be rejected because it is unnecessary, unsupported by evidence, outside the relevant level of government’s responsibility, unfair, too expensive compared with its benefit, likely to cause worse unintended consequences, or already covered by existing law. Rejecting one response does not necessarily mean ignoring the problem; a different response may be more effective.

Section G — The simplified pathway
  1. A public problem or policy idea is identified.
  2. Evidence is gathered.
  3. A government department develops advice.
  4. The responsible minister considers the proposal.
  5. Cabinet decides whether the government will support further action.
  6. If legislation is required, legal drafters prepare a Bill.
  7. The Bill is introduced into Parliament.

Reality check: A proposal may move backwards for more research, consultation or revision. Some action uses existing powers and never requires a Bill. The pathway is a useful map, not a rigid rule for every case.
