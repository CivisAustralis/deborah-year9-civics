What conditions must exist before the state may legitimately punish?

Justice concerns both process and outcome. A popular outcome cannot excuse an unfair process; equally, a carefully designed process reduces error but cannot guarantee factual perfection. Before punishment, the prosecution must overcome the presumption of innocence: an accused person is treated as not guilty unless guilt is proved according to law. This does not prevent a lawful investigation, arrest or trial.

Procedural fairness requires notice of allegations, access to adverse material, a genuine opportunity to answer and challenge it, consideration of relevant evidence, absence of prejudgment and reasons capable of review. The connected right to be heard must be meaningful, not merely ceremonial. These protections preserve the person’s rights and public confidence in the legitimacy of the decision.

Impartiality means a decision-maker does not favour a party because of prejudice, personal interest, media hostility, popularity or improper influence. Judicial independence protects courts institutionally from direction or intimidation by government, politicians, wealthy interests, campaigns or public anger. Independence does not make judges immune from informed criticism; it ensures criticism is not command.

Equality before the law means legal worth does not depend on wealth, race, religion, status or influence. As Lesson 3 showed, identical rules may operate unfairly where people have unequal access to representation, evidence or communication support. Consistency asks that similar cases follow similar principles, while still recognising relevant individual differences.

Proportionality demands a defensible relationship between punishment and the offence’s seriousness, the offender’s culpability or responsibility, intention, harm, planning, aggravating and mitigating factors, and rehabilitation prospects. It is more demanding than the slogan “the worst offence deserves the worst possible punishment”: the selected penalty itself must remain justified.

Open justice generally makes proceedings and reasons public, subject to lawful exceptions protecting children, vulnerable witnesses or confidential material. Visibility enables scrutiny, appeal, informed criticism and consistent reasoning. Rule of law means punishment is authorised and controlled by known law, not revenge, mob demand, secret decision, arbitrary command or political retaliation.

Review and appeal acknowledge possible mistakes: eyewitness identification can be mistaken; forensic evidence misunderstood; relevant material withheld; representation inadequate; directions incorrect; confessions unreliable; or new evidence discovered. A miscarriage of justice is a serious legal failure producing an unjust outcome. Exoneration formally clears a person; clemency or commutation may reduce or replace punishment without necessarily declaring the conviction wrong. Finally, humane treatment recognises that conviction does not remove every right or human dignity. Lawful punishment must not become arbitrary cruelty or degradation.

Expert submissions on punishment

Criminal sentences may pursue several purposes, but those purposes can pull in different directions. Permanent incapacitation can exclude rehabilitation; rapid denunciation can threaten careful review; severe retribution can conflict with mercy. The Commission must identify what it prioritises, the evidence supporting that choice and the rights placed at risk.

Professor Eleanor Ward — Sentencing scholar

“Retribution is a measured punitive response to culpable wrongdoing, not private vengeance. It recognises seriousness and responsibility through proportionality. Yet maximum severity is not automatically proportionate: intention, harm, planning, mitigation and individual responsibility must be considered. Denunciation publicly marks conduct as gravely unacceptable, but condemnation of an act does not logically prove that the harshest available penalty is required.”

Dr Amira Chen — Criminologist

“Specific deterrence seeks to discourage an individual; general deterrence seeks to discourage others. Deterrence is partly an empirical claim—a claim tested against observed evidence. Severity alone proves little. People may not know the penalty, expect detection or calculate rationally. Certainty of detection, speed of response and public knowledge may matter differently from severity.”

Democracy Sausage: “The harshest punishment must create the strongest deterrent. Bigger penalty, bigger fear, fewer offences.”

Classify the claim:

Deborah: The slogan is intuitive, but evidence must show how people decide, whether they expect detection and whether added severity changes behaviour.

Commissioner Daniel Okafor — Corrections administrator

“Incapacitation restricts opportunities to cause particular harm in the community. Secure imprisonment and risk management can provide community protection. Execution makes incapacitation permanent, but also makes punishment permanently uncorrectable. A responsible policy compares the protection offered by available alternatives rather than assuming only one severe penalty can protect.”

Dr Elizabeth Morales — Rehabilitation researcher

“Rehabilitation seeks to lower future offending through education, treatment, insight, responsibility, behavioural change, skills, supervision and stable relationships. It is not a promise that everyone will change. Execution eliminates every later possibility of rehabilitation—and every opportunity to respond if the conviction is overturned.”

Ms Priya Nair — Restorative justice practitioner

“Restoration may involve acknowledgment of harm, responsibility, victim participation, repair where possible and community relationships. It cannot undo death or serious harm, is not suitable in identical form for every case and does not erase accountability. Victim voice matters, but it does not by itself settle which legal penalty is proportionate. Capital punishment is chiefly retributive and incapacitative, not restorative.”

Faith & Citizenship — Justice, Mercy and State Power

Christian ethical traditions have emphasised justice, accountability, protection of innocent life, mercy, dignity, repentance, redemption and legitimate public authority. Christians have not reached one conclusion about capital punishment. Some argue that civil authority must punish grave wrongdoing, proportionate retribution affirms the seriousness of the victim’s loss, community protection matters and denunciation upholds responsibility. Others argue that dignity remains after wrongdoing, wrongful conviction makes execution morally intolerable, moral change should remain possible, secure imprisonment can protect, and state authority should be limited by mercy and humility.

The Book of Judges depicts repeated cycles of violence, retaliation, unstable leadership and social disorder. It does not provide a simple modern sentencing code. It can, however, prompt difficult questions about what happens when justice becomes indistinguishable from vengeance and when power lacks consistent, accountable institutions.

“Act justly, love mercy and walk humbly.” Why might these commitments become especially important when the state considers a punishment that can never be reversed?

This optional panel is ethical reflection, not Australian law. It does not prescribe one Christian conclusion, require victims to forgive, minimise wrongdoing or equate mercy with no accountability.
