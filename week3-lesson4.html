🐝 Mission 12 — The Justice Review Commission

A fictional national commission is reviewing the principles governing criminal punishment. Australia has abolished capital punishment, but the commission uses it as a demanding ethical case study: Can a punishment be just if an error can never be corrected?

As junior justice researcher, identify principles, distinguish punishment purposes, evaluate competing arguments, examine irreversible error and prepare a reasoned recommendation.

This is not a lesson in choosing the angriest or most compassionate answer. It asks whether a punishment can satisfy the principles by which a justice system claims to operate.

Content note: This lesson discusses capital punishment and wrongful conviction in a non-graphic way. You will not view or read graphic descriptions of executions or violent offences. The focus is justice, evidence, state power and legal safeguards.

📖 Investigation I — Principles of Justice

Section A — Justice as process and outcome

Justice can be evaluated by how a decision was made, whether rights were protected, whether evidence was tested, whether the decision-maker was impartial, whether punishment was proportionate and whether serious error could be corrected. A desired outcome cannot excuse an unfair process. Conversely, a carefully designed process reduces error but cannot guarantee that every factual conclusion is correct.

Section B — Procedural fairness

Procedural fairness requires notice of an allegation, access to the case being made, a genuine opportunity to respond, consideration of relevant evidence, absence of prejudgment and reasons capable of examination. It protects the individual from arbitrary power and strengthens public confidence that decisions emerge from law and evidence rather than predetermined conclusions.

Section C — Impartiality and judicial independence

Impartiality concerns a decision-maker’s neutrality in an individual case: no prejudice, personal interest or favour. Judicial independence is the institutional freedom of judges and courts from improper government, media or private direction. Intense publicity and public outrage may be understandable, but must not replace careful adjudication according to law.

Section D — Equality, presumption and the right to be heard

Equality before the law rejects improper differences based on wealth, race, religion, social status or political popularity. Identical rules can still have unequal effects where access to lawyers and evidence differs. The presumption of innocence treats an accused as not guilty unless prosecution proves guilt according to law. The right to be heard permits an adequate response and challenge to adverse evidence.

Section E — Proportionality and consistency

Proportionality asks whether punishment has a rational and morally defensible relationship to offence seriousness, intention, harm, planning, responsibility, aggravating and mitigating circumstances, prior conduct and rehabilitation prospects. It is more sophisticated than saying “the worst offence deserves the worst punishment.” Consistency applies similar principles to similar cases while recognising relevant individual differences.

Section F — Review, appeal and correction

Appeal and review recognise that witnesses, forensic interpretation, lawyers, judges and jury directions can be mistaken, and important evidence may emerge later. A miscarriage of justice is a serious legal failure producing an unjust outcome. Exoneration formally clears a person when a conviction or accusation is shown to be unsound. Clemency and commutation may reduce or replace a punishment, but do not necessarily declare a conviction wrong.

Section G — Open justice and rule of law

Open justice generally makes hearings and reasons public, enabling scrutiny, criticism, appeal, consistency and understanding. Lawful exceptions can protect children, vulnerable witnesses or confidential information. The rule of law requires public power to follow known law rather than personal revenge or arbitrary command.

Section H — Humane treatment and dignity

A justice system may condemn grave conduct while recognising that accused and convicted people retain legal rights and human dignity. Wrongdoing does not authorise arbitrary or degrading treatment. This principle creates difficult questions about punishment’s limits and whether state power should leave space for correction, rehabilitation, mercy and change.

📚 Investigation II — Purposes of Punishment

Section I — Retribution and denunciation

Retribution is a measured legal response reflecting culpable wrongdoing and responsibility; it is not personal vengeance. Denunciation publicly expresses society’s condemnation. Supporters of capital punishment may argue that exceptionally serious intentional killing warrants the most severe response. A further question remains: does powerful denunciation require the most severe available penalty?

Section J — Deterrence

Specific deterrence seeks to discourage the offender; general deterrence seeks to discourage others. Whether one penalty deters more than another is an empirical claim requiring credible evidence, not assumption. Severity, likelihood of detection, certainty, speed and public awareness may operate differently. Evidence cannot conclusively answer every moral question, but policy claims about behaviour require evidence.

Section K — Incapacitation and protection

Incapacitation restricts an offender’s ability to cause particular harms in the general community. Secure imprisonment can protect the community for long periods. Capital punishment permanently incapacitates, but also makes correction impossible once carried out. Protection and irreversibility therefore must be analysed together rather than treated as unrelated concerns.

Section L — Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation seeks to reduce future offending through insight, education, treatment, self-control, employability and constructive relationships. Capital punishment excludes rehabilitation. Supporters may contend that some conduct forfeits this opportunity; opponents may argue that the state should not deny every possibility of change or later exoneration.

Section M — Restoration

Restoration may acknowledge harm, encourage responsibility, include victim voice and repair loss or relationships where possible. It is not suitable for every case and cannot erase grave harm. Capital punishment is mainly retributive and incapacitative, rather than restorative. Mercy does not necessarily mean absence of accountability.

Section N — Purposes can conflict

Permanent incapacitation conflicts with rehabilitation; severe retribution can conflict with mercy; rapid punishment can conflict with careful review; denunciation can generate pressure threatening impartiality. Sophisticated policy identifies which purposes take priority, why, what evidence supports them and which rights or values may be compromised.

Section O — Competing arguments

Arguments supporting capital punishment commonly invoke retribution, denunciation, permanent incapacitation, deterrence, proportionality and legitimate state authority. Arguments opposing it invoke irreversible error, miscarriages of justice, unequal application, dignity and right to life, lost rehabilitation, arbitrary influence, uncertain added deterrence, and tension with mercy or redemption. These include different kinds of claims; they are not all equally supported merely because each can be stated.

🕰️ Australian Historical Briefing

  1. 1967Ronald Ryan was executed in Victoria, the last person executed in Australia.
  2. 1973The Commonwealth Death Penalty Abolition Act 1973 abolished capital punishment for Commonwealth and Territory offences within its scope; it did not instantly remove every state provision.
  3. 1985By this year, every Australian jurisdiction had abolished capital punishment.
  4. 1990Australia became bound by the Second Optional Protocol to the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, directed towards abolition.
  5. 2010Commonwealth legislation extended the national prohibition so no state or territory could reintroduce capital punishment.
  6. NowAustralia cannot impose capital punishment, is abolitionist, has international obligations directed towards abolition and opposes the death penalty as national policy. International cooperation in possible death-penalty cases engages legal and policy safeguards.

Abolition does not treat serious offences lightly. Australian courts may impose lengthy or life imprisonment where legislation permits. The policy is that the state must not execute a person as criminal punishment.

✝️ Faith & Citizenship — Justice, Mercy and the Power to Punish

Christian thought includes commitments to justice, accountability, protection of innocent life, mercy, dignity, repentance, redemption and legitimate public authority. Christians have not always reached one conclusion about capital punishment.

Some argue that civil authority must punish grave wrongdoing, proportionate retribution affirms the victim’s value, and severe punishment protects the community and denounces evil. Others argue that dignity remains after wrongdoing, wrongful conviction makes an irreversible sentence morally intolerable, repentance should remain possible, and secure imprisonment removes any need for the state deliberately to take life.

The Book of Judges depicts cycles of violence, retaliation, unstable leadership and disorder. It is not a modern sentencing code, but prompts questions about justice becoming indistinguishable from vengeance and power lacking accountable institutions. Micah 6:8 offers a brief ethical frame: “Act justly, love mercy and walk humbly.”

Why might justice, mercy and humility become especially important when the state considers an irreversible punishment?

This is ethical reflection, not binding Australian law. It does not impose one Christian conclusion, require victims to forgive, minimise serious crime or make mercy the absence of accountability.
